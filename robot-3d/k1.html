Learning Robust Humanoid Locomotion: Sim-to-Real Push Recovery on Booster K1

A PPO walking policy for the 19.7 kg Booster K1, fine-tuned to recover from shoves to the trunk with a push curriculum and domain randomization in mjlab (MuJoCo), trained on NCSA Delta GPUs, and run on the physical robot.

Reward design

The walking policy is trained on 15 reward terms. Seven are course-specified terms that each student implements in batched PyTorch (the list on the resume); the other eight are provided shaping and safety terms. The implementations are graded coursework and are withheld from the public repository, so this section gives the math and weights rather than code. Each term is multiplied by its weight and the 0.02 s control step.

The seven implemented terms (c = command, v = body-frame velocity, g = gravity in the body frame, at = raw action)

Weights are the course specification. A later revision on the rewards-v2-push branch raised standing pose to −2.0. Provided terms: body angular velocity (−0.01), whole-body angular momentum (−0.005), joint limits (−1.0), foot clearance (−2.0), swing height (−0.25), soft landing (curriculum), self-collision (−1.0), upper-body posture (−0.1). The push tasks add a fall penalty (−400 × 0.02 s = −8 per fall) in the ladder task.

Disturbance model

Each push is one force on the trunk, held 0.05–0.15 s, followed by 3–6 s to recover. The sampled quantity is the impulse J (N·s); the force follows from the duration, so a short push is a jab and a long one a shove with the same effect on momentum. A 50 N·s push on the 19.7 kg robot is a velocity change of about 2.5 m/s.

J ~ U(J_lo, J_hi)  [N·s]        Δt ~ U(0.05, 0.15) s        |F| = J / Δt
direction ψ ~ U(0, 2π)   tilt θ ~ U(−20°, 20°)   (relative to the robot's heading)
F = R_yaw · |F| · (cos θ cos ψ, cos θ sin ψ, sin θ)
contact r = h·ẑ + s·(side unit vector),  h ~ U(0, 12 cm) above the trunk CoM,  s ~ U(−7, 7) cm
torque τ = r × F        (applied with F at the trunk CoM)

Impulse, not peak force, decided whether the baseline fell: in a 64-robot check, 300 N for 0.1 s and 200 N for 0.15 s (both 30 N·s) knocked over about the same share. So the curriculum ramps impulse directly. The course's small velocity kicks (up to 0.28 m/s) stay on, spaced to every 3–6 s.

# k1_push/mdp/events.py (trunk_impulse.__call__, abridged)
impulse   = sample_uniform(*impulse_range, n, dev)        # N·s, the ramped quantity
duration  = sample_uniform(*duration_s, n, dev)
size      = impulse / duration                           # force (N)
direction = torch.rand(n, device=dev) * 2 * math.pi
tilt      = torch.deg2rad(sample_uniform(*tilt_deg, n, dev))
unit      = torch.stack((tilt.cos() * direction.cos(), tilt.cos() * direction.sin(), tilt.sin()), dim=-1)
force_w   = quat_apply(yaw_quat(root_quat_w), unit) * size[:, None]
offset_w  = quat_apply(heading, height[:, None] * up + lateral[:, None] * side)
torque_w  = torch.cross(offset_w, force_w, dim=-1)       # off-centre pushes twist the trunk

Experiments and results simulation

Protocol

  • Policies fine-tuned from the HW3 walking checkpoint (model_20999, the one already running on the robot).
  • scripts/eval_push_falls.py: 256 robots walk on the play commands; each receives exactly one trunk push drawn from a fixed impulse range (random direction, tilt and contact point, as in training). Velocity kicks are off, so the push is the only disturbance. A robot counts as fallen if the tilt termination fires within 2 s of the push. Robots that fall before their push are excluded. Every checkpoint sees the same seed.
  • Checkpoints stage0–stage3 are the fixed-curriculum run sampled at the end of each stage.

Findings. stage1 cut falls at 15–40 N·s from 39% to 13% (recovery 61% → 87%) without ever training at that level. Continuing the same schedule undid it: more aggressive curricula fell below the untrained baseline (stage3 at every level). Each cell is one run and one seed, so differences of a few points (for example stage0 vs stage1 at 15–40 N·s) are within run-to-run noise; no confidence intervals are claimed.

Failure analysis and the adaptive curriculum

What the fall test showed

The fall-rate table above is the evidence for the collapse: each extra stage of the fixed schedule past 10–30 N·s made the policy worse at every test level, and stage3 ended below the untrained baseline (6 / 27 / 46 / 65% falls against 1 / 12 / 39 / 51%).

What the training logs showed

  • The illegal_contact termination (a non-foot body touching the ground) stepped up at each curriculum stage switch and did not come back down within a stage.
  • The policy's action standard deviation (Policy/mean_std) rose from 0.41 to 0.63 over the later stages, so PPO was not settling on an improvement.

I traced the collapse in the training logs to illegal-contact terminations spiking at each curriculum stage while action noise rose: at 15–40 and 20–50 N·s too many training pushes could not be survived at all, so they carried no useful learning signal and drowned out the pushes that could. The rising action noise meant PPO had no consistent improvement to commit to, not that it lacked exploration. Source: experiment log, PROGRESS.md, Nov 2 entry.

Survival-gated ladder

Instead of switching on a fixed iteration count, the push only gets harder once the robot survives it.

  • 13 levels from 5–20 to 20–50 N·s: the four test ranges plus three evenly spaced steps between each.
  • Every 200 iterations, with at least 3,000 pushes counted: step up if survival ≥ 85%, step down if < 70%, otherwise hold.
  • A push is survived if no fall or illegal contact happens within 2 s of it (the benchmark's definition; time-outs are not falls).
  • The level is written atomically to a JSON state file, so a 4-hour job restart resumes on the same level.
  • A fall penalty (weight −400, i.e. −8 per fall) makes falling cost about twice what it did.
# k1_push/mdp/adaptive.py (adaptive_push_curriculum.__call__, abridged)
total    = term.n_survived + term.n_fell
survival = term.n_survived / total.clamp(min=1)
if now - self._mark >= dwell_steps and int(total.item()) >= min_pushes:
    rate, old = float(survival.item()), self.level
    if rate >= up and self.level < len(self._ladder) - 1:     # up = 0.85
        self.level += 1
    elif rate < down and self.level > 0:                      # down = 0.70
        self.level -= 1
    self.history.append([int(now), old, self.level, round(rate, 4), int(total.item())])
    self._apply(); self._save()                               # impulse_range ← ladder[level]; JSON for resumes
    self._mark = now; term.clear_counts()

Before it ran on the real simulator, the counting and level logic was tested on a mock push/fall process with known fall rates (measured 95/90/70% survival against 95/90/70% set; it climbed, held at 70%, resumed at the saved level after a restart, and climbed again once survival reached 84%).

Outcome. The survival-gated ladder avoided the collapse: survival stayed above 79% at every decision and the ladder never had to step down, where the fixed schedule had held the robot at levels it mostly could not survive.

Training infrastructure

  • Multi-GPU training jobs on NVIDIA A100, A40 and GH200 GPUs on NCSA Delta and DeltaAI through Slurm, with local runs on an RTX 3050 laptop.
  • 4,096 parallel environments, batched on the GPU, reaching ~400,000 environment steps per second on GH200. Jobs run under a 4-hour limit, with several (seeds, tasks, evals) side by side.
  • Runs are chains of 4-hour jobs that resume from the last checkpoint (saved every 50 iterations). The push curriculum and the ladder were written to survive that: the stage clock counts from the parent checkpoint's step, and the ladder level lives in a state file.
  • Every task is checked against its parent by run.py check before it trains: the push task must equal the baseline except for the push, DR must equal the push task except for the widened ranges, and so on.
  • Logging to Weights & Biases; checkpoints exported to ONNX with Booster's metadata for deployment.

Sim-to-real deployment hardware

Safety and method

  • The robot ran on a safety gantry, with a spotter on the strap; pushes were by hand, to the trunk.
  • Launched with --exit-mode damping: if the deploy tool's fall stop fires (projected gravity z > −0.5, about 60° of tilt) the motors go limp and the gantry catches the robot, instead of Booster's own walking controller taking over and producing a "save" that is not ours.
  • Before the A press, Custom mode runs Booster's warm-up walker, not our policy. Pushes were only counted after A.
  • Each run was recorded as a ROS 2 bag (/robot_states, /fall_down, /low_state, /remote_controller_state, /joint_ctrl).
  • The bags were checked afterwards: who had control at every instant (our policy runs only in mode 3 / body control 6), the K1's own fall detector, the largest gap in /joint_ctrl (60–78 ms in every run), and trunk tilt from the IMU, tilt = acos(cos roll · cos pitch).

Arm flailing and a crouched gait

On hardware the policies walk with a crouched gait (knees deeply bent) and the arms flail during recovery. I traced both to reward design: the upper-body posture and whole-body angular-momentum terms carry very small weights in the walking reward (−0.1 and −0.005), so nothing pushes the policy toward a tall stance or quiet arms. Proposed fix, not yet trained: upper-body posture and angular-momentum terms strong enough to shape the gait.

Limitations and next steps

  • Hardware pushes were not calibrated. No force or impulse was measured, about 18 pushes over 4 runs, one session. Every policy held every push, so the hardware data cannot rank them. The planned comparison is 3 policies × 4 directions × 3 repeatable levels × 3 repeats (108 trials) with a fixed-swing push stick.
  • Single seed. Each simulated result is one training run.
  • DR vs no DR on DR physics is the next simulated comparison (same fall test, --task K1-Push-DR-Flat-v0).
